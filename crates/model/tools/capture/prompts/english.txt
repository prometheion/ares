The tide-predicting machine was one of the most elegant analogue computers ever built. Lord Kelvin designed the first one in 1872, and for almost a century harbour authorities relied on such machines to publish their annual tables. Each machine carried a set of pulleys, one for every tidal constituent: the pull of the moon, the pull of the sun, the elliptical shape of the lunar orbit, and dozens of smaller effects. A single wire ran over all the pulleys, and a pen at the end of the wire drew the height of the water against the passing hours. To predict a year of tides at one port, the operator first set each pulley to the amplitude and phase measured there, then turned a crank for a few days. Nothing in the machine knew about the sea. It only knew that the sea behaves like a sum of regular waves, and that a sum of regular waves can be added with string. When electronic computers arrived, the same calculation took seconds, yet the principle stayed the same: find the few periodic motions that explain most of the signal, and ignore the rest until the data demand otherwise.
